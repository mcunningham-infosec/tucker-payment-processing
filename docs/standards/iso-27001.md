# ISO/IEC 27001:2022
The information security management system behind the site: clauses 4 to 10 and the Annex A controls · Self-assessment · November 25, 2026

## About this document
This is a self-assessment written by Michael Cunningham, who designed and built the site. It is not a certification: only an accredited certification body can issue one. ISO/IEC 27001 is the international standard for an information security management system (ISMS). The current edition is ISO/IEC 27001:2022, with Amendment 1 (2024), which asks organizations to consider whether climate change is relevant to their security. Certificates to the old 2013 edition stopped being valid on October 31, 2025.
The standard has two parts. Clauses 4 to 10 describe the management system: scope, leadership, risk assessment, operation, measurement and improvement. Annex A lists 93 controls in four themes: organizational (37), people (8), physical (14) and technological (34). ISO/IEC 42001, the AI management system standard, is built on the same structure, so the two fit together; the AI side is covered in the ISO/IEC 42001 write-up.
Evidence: `docs/standards/iso-42001.md`.

## The management system (clauses 4 to 10)
### Clause 4: Context and scope
**Status: Met**
The scope is the website, its API, its two AI systems, its database and the server and cloud services that run them. The interested parties are visitors, the people who test it, the vendors and the owner. On climate (Amendment 1), it was considered and judged not relevant to the site's security beyond the choice of hosting provider.
Evidence: `README.md`, `SECURITY.md`.

### Clause 5: Leadership
**Status: Partly met**
There is one accountable owner, a published security policy and published company policies, and security is designed in rather than added later. A one-person company cannot show what an auditor would test in a larger one: management commitment across a team and assigned security roles.
Evidence: `SECURITY.md`, `policies/`.

### Clause 6: Planning and risk assessment
**Status: Partly met**
Risks are identified and treated in writing: the AI risks in the ISO/IEC 23894 and NIST write-ups, the web and AI attacks in the OWASP write-ups, and the payment risks in the PCI DSS write-up, each linked to the control that answers it. What is missing is a single formal Statement of Applicability that lists all 93 Annex A controls with a decision for each; this document covers them by theme.
Evidence: `docs/standards/iso-23894.md`, `docs/standards/owasp-llm-top-10.md`, `docs/standards/pci-dss.md`.

### Clause 7: Support
**Status: Met**
Documented information is kept in version control, so every policy, configuration and control has a history. Policies are published, and the owner brings 20+ years of security leadership to the work.
Evidence: `policies/`, `deploy/README.md`.

### Clause 8: Operation
**Status: Met**
The planned controls run every day: automated updates, nightly data resets, a nightly security report, real-time alerts and spend caps. Changes are planned and deployed by one scripted command.
Evidence: `deploy/auto-maintenance.sh`, `db/nightly_reset.sql`, `app/report.py`, `deploy/update.sh`.

### Clause 9: Performance evaluation
**Status: Partly met**
Security is measured continuously: automated tests, five independent public scanners, the attack simulator's 50 live attacks and an outside-in check that the files the live site serves match the published code. A certification auditor would also expect a formal internal audit programme and management reviews on a set schedule, which do not exist here.
Evidence: `tests/`, `docs/scans/`, `ops/live_watch.py`.

### Clause 10: Improvement
**Status: Met**
Problems are fixed and the fix is tested: the three gaps the first round of public scans found were closed the same day, the findings still open are recorded with the scan results, and each defense that failed during an attack run is flagged for a human to fix.
Evidence: `docs/scans/`, `app/cipher/soc.py`.

## Annex A controls
### Organizational controls (A.5)
**Status: Partly met**
Policies for information security, acceptable use of AI, data retention and card security are written and published (A.5.1, A.5.10). Threat intelligence comes from OWASP and MITRE ATLAS, and the attack simulator turns it into tests (A.5.7). Suppliers and cloud services are known and limited to what each needs (A.5.19 to A.5.23). Incidents are detected, alerted, investigated by the AI SOC and fixed (A.5.24 to A.5.28). The gap is segregation of duties (A.5.3): one person builds, approves and deploys.
Evidence: `policies/`, `app/cipher/attacks.py`, `app/alerts.py`, `site/about.html`.

### People controls (A.6)
**Status: Not applicable**
Screening, employment terms, awareness training and disciplinary processes apply to staff, and the company has none. The one people control that does apply, remote working (A.6.7), is covered under access control below: all administration goes through Google's identity-checked tunnel with two-step verification.
Evidence: `deploy/gcp-firewall.md`.

### Physical controls (A.7)
**Status: Not applicable**
The company has no office or data center. Physical security is inherited from Google Cloud, whose data centers hold their own ISO/IEC 27001 certification. A real company would keep Google's certificate on file as supplier evidence.

### Access control and authentication (A.8.2 to A.8.5)
**Status: Met**
There is no public SSH and no admin port open to the internet; admin access, including all remote administration (A.6.7), needs a Google identity with two-step verification. Database logins use the operating system's identity, with no passwords to steal, and each part of the app gets only the access it needs: the chat reads, and the store can call one function. The AI chatbot can only read a customer's data after the identity check.
Evidence: `deploy/gcp-firewall.md`, `db/roles.sql`, `deploy/postgres-store-login.md`, `app/tools.py`.

### Malware, vulnerabilities and configuration (A.8.7 to A.8.9)
**Status: Met**
The server installs security updates itself and restarts cleanly. Dependabot watches every library for known vulnerabilities and malware, and the server's hardening (security headers, blocked methods, hidden files) is kept in version control and re-checked by the attack simulator.
Evidence: `deploy/auto-maintenance.sh`, `deploy/apache-hardening.conf`, `requirements.txt`.

### Deletion, masking and leak prevention (A.8.10 to A.8.12)
**Status: Met**
Web logs are deleted after two days and the demo data resets every night. Anything in the AI's reply that looks like a full card number is masked before it is shown, and no full card number exists anywhere in the system.
Evidence: `deploy/logrotate-apache2`, `db/nightly_reset.sql`, `app/guardrails.py`, `db/schema.sql`.

### Backup and redundancy (A.8.13 to A.8.14)
**Status: Partly met**
Everything needed to rebuild the site, from code and configuration to the database schema and demo data, is in the repository, and the data restores itself to a known state every night. There is one server and no standby, which is acceptable for a demonstration but would not meet a commitment to customers.
Evidence: `db/schema.sql`, `db/seed.sql`, `deploy/README.md`.

### Logging and monitoring (A.8.15 to A.8.16)
**Status: Met**
Web requests, guardrail events and blocked attacks are logged. Detection rules raise an alert after repeated attacks from one source and refuse that source for the rest of the run after 50; real-time alerts reach the owner by email, and a nightly report summarizes the day. Logs are kept briefly on purpose, for privacy, which limits how far back an investigation can look.
Evidence: `app/cipher/detect.py`, `app/alerts.py`, `deploy/alert-policy.json`, `app/report.py`.

### Network security and cryptography (A.8.20 to A.8.24)
**Status: Met**
The firewall admits web traffic only from Cloudflare, which adds a web application firewall and bot defense. Only TLS 1.2 and 1.3 are accepted, HSTS is preloaded, and the domain uses DNSSEC and CAA. Independent scanners rated the configuration A+.
Evidence: `deploy/gcp-firewall.md`, `deploy/apache-cloudflare.conf`, `deploy/dns.md`, `docs/scans/`.

### Secure development and testing (A.8.25 to A.8.29)
**Status: Met**
Security is part of the design from the start. Every change on GitHub is scanned by CodeQL and secret scanning, more than 80 automated tests check the security behavior, and the attack simulator runs 50 real attacks against the live site. Secrets never enter the repository.
Evidence: `tests/`, `app/cipher/attacks.py`, `README.md`.

### Change management (A.8.32)
**Status: Partly met**
Every change is recorded in GitHub and deployed by one scripted command that waits for any attack run in progress to finish. The gap is independent approval: the builder also approves and deploys, where an auditor would expect a second reviewer.
Evidence: `deploy/update.sh`.

## The bottom line
The technical controls are strong, because they are built into code and configuration rather than left to people. The gaps are the ones any one-person company has: no segregation of duties, no internal audit or management review cycle, and no formal Statement of Applicability. A company preparing for certification would close those first.
